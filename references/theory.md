# Theory: how AI remembers, how humans remember, and what follows

Use this file to explain the "why" to the user — in simple words, in pieces, not all at once.

## 1. AI vs humans: the real difference

A common impression: "AI absorbs information instantly, while a human needs months." That comparison is wrong.

- **Inside a conversation, an AI learns nothing.** Everything it was told sits in its context — like a cheat sheet that's always in front of it. When the conversation ends, the information is gone; the model itself hasn't changed.
- **Real AI learning is slow and expensive.** A model is trained on trillions of words, for months, on huge compute clusters. A person can often grasp a new concept from just a few examples. In learning efficiency, humans win by a wide margin.
- **Humans don't have a cheat sheet.** Every time, they have to pull knowledge out of their own neural connections and assemble it piece by piece. That's why they need to learn it, not just see it.

In one line: **AI holds instantly but doesn't learn; a human only hears instantly and learns over time.**

## 2. What physically happens in the brain

1. New information first forms a fragile trace in the **hippocampus**, where it lives for days to weeks.
2. To become long-term, it's transferred to the **cortex** — this is called consolidation. The transfer happens mostly **during sleep**, gradually, over several nights.
3. Consolidation **physically rewires synapses**, which requires protein synthesis. That's why the process can't be instant.
4. A trace may consolidate well, partially (a vague "this feels familiar"), or fade — depending on how many times and how it was retrieved.
5. New knowledge attaches to existing connections. If there's already a base in the field, the new material has something to hook onto — that's why **an expert learns new things in their field faster than a beginner**.

Takeaway: you can't speed up the biology, but you can make every contact with the material count.

## 3. What works best

| Method | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Active recall** (retelling in your own words without looking, answering questions) | The strongest method. The brain strengthens what it retrieves; the effort of retrieval is the training itself. |
| **Attempting an answer before the explanation** | "Warms up" the brain: activates related knowledge and exposes the gap, so the answer lands in a prepared spot. Works even when the attempt is wrong. |
| **Spaced repetition** (1–3 days, a week, ~3 weeks) | Reviewing once you've partly forgotten forces effortful retrieval and strengthens the trace far more than repeating back to back. |
| **Explaining simply (Feynman technique)** | Forces you to rebuild the knowledge in your own structure. Where it won't come out simply, there's a gap in understanding. |
| **Applying it within 24 hours** | Knowledge used in a real task gets tied to action and context — it sticks better. |
| **Sleep** | It's a processing stage, not a break from it: transfer to long-term memory happens at night. |
| **Linking to what you know** | New hooks onto old; the more anchor points, the faster and firmer. |
| **Interleaving topics and problems** | The brain learns to tell when to use what, not just repeat a single template. |

## 4. What works worst

| What people do | Why it barely works |
|---|---|
| **Rereading** | Creates an illusion of knowing: the text feels familiar, but you can't retrieve it without a prompt. Recognition is not recall. |
| **"I got it while listening"** | Understanding during an explanation is not the same as being able to reproduce it. Without recall, the trace fades fast. |
| **Passive note-copying and highlighting** | Rewriting without processing it in your own words adds almost nothing to memory. |
| **Cramming in one day** | Works briefly for "tomorrow's test", but without spacing and sleep it's quickly forgotten. |
| **Repeating right after learning "to be safe"** | The material is still in working memory — there's nothing to retrieve, so no training. |
| **Sleep deprivation** | Disrupts consolidation: what you learned that day transfers to long-term memory worse. |
| **Many topics, no application** | The knowledge isn't tied to action or to each other — nothing holds it in place. |

## 5. A practical workflow with AI

1. Form your own hypothesis or answer.
2. Ask the AI.
3. Close the answer and retell it in your own words.
4. Ask the AI to give you 3–5 questions and check your answers.
5. Close the gaps — re-answer only where you were wrong.
6. Apply it within 24 hours.
7. After 2–3 days and after a week, answer the same questions without hints (on a reminder).

It takes a little longer than just reading the answer, but it saves weeks: the information doesn't evaporate, it stays in your head in a usable form.
